Thanks for having me, Jenn. I've been a designer for about ten years, the last five focused on UX and UI, and most recently as a senior product designer. I specialize in data-heavy workflows with multiple user and account types, mostly in environmental and regulatory fields, like artisanal small-scale mining and carbon projects.

What drew me to Toybox is how easy you made the whole 3D experience feel. During my degree I had to learn Blender, before AI, and I remember how complex the logic was. Making that simple enough for a kid to play with is a real design achievement, and I'd love to be part of that.

I'm also curious where the product goes from here, whether that's bringing in adults or not losing the kid who grew up.

I don't think the core problem changes much. In B2B, people use the tool because their job requires it, so the win is speed and clarity. In consumer, nobody owes you their attention, so the win is making complexity disappear entirely.

What carries over is designing for more than one user at once. In my last role that was the operator entering data and the decision maker reading it. Here it's the kid creating and the parent paying and trusting. Same challenge, different faces.

I haven't shipped a consumer mobile product professionally, but I've been building one on the side, a self-care app with a 3D avatar of myself.

Straatos, a carbon market platform. They came with a rough sketch they weren't happy with, and years of work living in spreadsheets.

First I studied how they actually entered data. Then I told them we needed to translate everything into platform language, more granular data points we could measure over time. We did that together.

Two results. We could give them analytics they didn't know were possible. And their certification documents used to take months to write. Now the data fills in as people work, day by day. Their investor pitch got much stronger, and investor conversion went up over the past year.

I'm used to that. When there's no brief, I start by digging: documentation, old files, Slack threads. Then I talk to whoever was involved, because a lot of the context lives in people's heads.

For prioritizing, I look at three things. What comes up repeatedly, where people are actually getting stuck, and what unblocks other work downstream.

Then I don't wait for direction, I propose one. I'd rather show up with a prototype and be wrong than ask an open question and stall everyone. As a solo designer you don't win arguments with authority, you win them with something concrete.

First I check if the disagreement is real, or just different vocabulary for the same thing.

If it's real, I ask each side what they're optimizing for. Then it becomes a trade-off conversation instead of a fight.

If everything is too expensive, that usually means we're scoped too big, so I look for the smallest version that tests the assumption.

Whatever we decide, I make sure people know why.

It depends on the goal, so the first thing is setting the target before we design. If we want fewer people abandoning a task, we measure completion. If it's a new subscription flow, we measure conversion.

When there's no clean number, I go qualitative: watching people use it, reviews, support tickets.
